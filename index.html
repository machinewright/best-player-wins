    Why the best player loses so often

    Take the starting settings: sixteen players, and your best one beats a middle-of-the-pack player four times out of five. Against the second-best player, that edge shrinks to 60%. A knockout asks your best player to win four games in a row, the last ones against the strongest opposition. They manage it in about 35% of tournaments. Nearly two times out of three, the trophy goes to someone else.

    Formats that forgive one bad game do better. Double elimination lets everyone lose once, and the best player's chance goes up to just over 40%. A round robin, where everyone plays everyone, gets to about 50%, but it takes fifteen rounds. Even then, your best player loses half of all tournaments. One event is a small sample, and no format can change that much.

    Seeding, putting the strongest players far apart in the draw, helps less than people expect. In the same setting, a knockout with names drawn from a hat crowns the best player 32% of the time; with perfect seeding, 37%.

    The Swiss surprise

    A Swiss tournament pairs players with the same score each round, so nobody is knocked out. The usual advice is to play as many rounds as it takes to halve the field down to one: four rounds for sixteen players. With sixteen players, four rounds and no draws, exactly one player wins every game, and that player wins the tournament. That is the same rule as a knockout. The difference is that everyone else keeps playing. So a Swiss with the usual rounds gives everyone more games, but it doesn't pick a better winner: about 32% in the starting settings, against 35% for a knockout. Add rounds and it improves, slowly:

    With draws, as in chess, more than one player can finish on top, and the tie-break rules start to matter. Choose "Draws: often" above to see the effect. One more oddity: seeding a Swiss by exact strength makes it slightly worse at crowning the best player (30% against 32% with a random order in the starting settings), because the top players meet each other sooner.

    What's behind the numbers

    Players. Your group is modelled as players of evenly spread strength, from best to weakest, as in a typical club. The slider sets how far apart they are. Each game is won with the probability given by the usual rating formula (Elo, or Bradley–Terry): the bigger the gap, the likelier the stronger player wins. Draws, when switched on, are most frequent between equal players. Nobody has good or bad days beyond that.

    Formats. Knockouts use the standard bracket (seed 1 and seed 2 can only meet in the final; byes go to the top seeds). Double elimination has a losers' bracket and a grand final, replayed once if the player from the losers' bracket wins it. Swiss pairs players on the same score, top half against bottom half, never twice the same opponents if it can avoid it; first place is decided by the Buchholz tie-break (the sum of your opponents' scores). Groups have three to five players; the top two of each go into a seeded knockout. Round robin ties are broken by the Sonneborn–Berger score. Any tie still left is settled by a playoff, so every tournament has exactly one winner, and "the best player wins" means they take the trophy outright. "Roughly" known strength means you would rank your players with about as much error as there is spread between them.

    Checking. Each figure comes from 4,000 simulated tournaments, so it is accurate to about ±1.5 percentage points. The simulation was checked against exact calculations: knockouts by working through the bracket, small round robins by listing every possible set of results. The code and tests are on GitHub.
